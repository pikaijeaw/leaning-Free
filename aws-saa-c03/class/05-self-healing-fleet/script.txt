A: Lesson five. Servers get sick. What if they healed themselves?
B: Sounds like magic.
A: It's two pieces. An Application Load Balancer, sitting in your public subnets. And behind it, an Auto Scaling group, a fleet of servers spread across zones in your private subnets.
B: So the load balancer hands out the traffic.
A: Right, and it only sends people to healthy servers. The Auto Scaling group keeps the count right. You give it three numbers. Minimum, desired, maximum.
B: And if a server dies?
A: The group notices it's short and launches a replacement. No human involved.
B: And when traffic spikes?
A: You pick a target metric, often average CPU. The group adds servers when it's above, and removes them when it's below.
B: Do sources agree on this?
A: Four of the six pages we read on load balancing and scaling teach this exact layout, and none disagree.
B: Got a real one?
A: KodeKloud has a lab that does both tests. First, they terminate an instance on purpose. The group's activity log says "Instance terminated", then "Launching a new instance".
B: [laughs] It just shrugs and replaces it.
A: Then they set a target of forty percent CPU, log into a server, and run a tool called stress to burn CPU. Desired capacity climbs from one to three.
B: And keeps going?
A: Stops at three, because three was the maximum. That's your safety cap on the bill.
B: Nice. Thing to try?
A: Fifteen minutes, on paper. A small online shop needs two servers on a normal day and six at its busiest. Write min, desired, max, and pick a target metric.

## T02 CIA triad and core principles
- d:N15. Definitions: confidentiality keeps data to authorized people; integrity keeps it accurate and unaltered; availability keeps it reachable by authorized users when needed.
  - Source: https://www.portnox.com/cybersecurity-101/general-security/cia-triad/  Quote: "accessible to authorized users whenever needed"
  - Kind: definition
  - Level: foundation
  - Bias: Portnox sells network access control; page is generic.
- d:N16. Which leg matters most depends on context: confidentiality for health records, integrity for bank transactions, availability for a hospital or power grid where downtime can be life-threatening. A good exam framing: name the asset, then the leg.
  - Source: https://www.portnox.com/cybersecurity-101/general-security/cia-triad/  Quote: "the most important part of the CIA Triad is context-dependent"
  - Kind: rule of thumb
  - Level: foundation
  - Also: T03
- d:N17. Control-to-leg mapping: encryption, RBAC, MFA serve confidentiality; hashing, digital signatures, version control serve integrity; redundant data centers, backups, DDoS protection serve availability.
  - Source: https://www.portnox.com/cybersecurity-101/general-security/cia-triad/  Quote: "If the hash changes during transmission, the integrity of the data has been compromised."
  - Kind: template
  - Level: foundation
  - Also: T04

## T07 Business continuity and disaster recovery
- d:N19. Three plans, three jobs: business continuity keeps critical operations running during any disruption; disaster recovery restores IT as fast as possible; incident response detects, contains and manages a security incident.
  - Source: https://www.techtarget.com/cybersecurity/tip/Business-continuity-vs-disaster-recovery-vs-incident-response  Quote: "Digital resilience and recovery depend on three pillars"
  - Kind: definition
  - Level: foundation
  - Also: T16
- d:N20. A BCP includes a cross-functional team, the list of critical business functions, the minimum resources (people, tech, third parties) to keep running, and at least an annual mock exercise. Third-party systems going offline must be in scope.
  - Source: https://www.techtarget.com/cybersecurity/tip/Business-continuity-vs-disaster-recovery-vs-incident-response  Quote: "Identify the minimum number of required resources to keep the business operational."
  - Kind: template
  - Level: core
- d:N21. A DRP includes frequent backups that are periodically restored to prove they work, audits of IT assets against inventory, and named recovery roles. A backup never test-restored is a hope, not a plan.
  - Source: https://www.techtarget.com/cybersecurity/tip/Business-continuity-vs-disaster-recovery-vs-incident-response  Quote: "periodically restored to confirm backup systems accurately mirror data"
  - Kind: test
  - Level: core
- d:N22. Shared best practices: write the plans while calm, name roles and current contact details, test at least yearly with mock crises, update often. "A plan that appears perfect on paper often has critical gaps in practice."
  - Source: https://www.techtarget.com/cybersecurity/tip/Business-continuity-vs-disaster-recovery-vs-incident-response  Quote: "Fail to make a disaster recovery plan, and plan to fail"
  - Kind: rule of thumb
  - Level: foundation
  - Also: T16

## T09 Access control models, least privilege, separation of duties
- d:N23. MAC: a central authority sets labels (classification plus compartment) on resources and clearances on users; access only when labels match; users cannot override. Born in military and intelligence; banks may use it for account data. Cost: hard to collaborate, heavy label management.
  - Source: https://www.twingate.com/blog/other/access-control-models  Quote: "A user may only access a resource if their security label matches"
  - Kind: definition
  - Level: core
  - Bias: Twingate sells zero trust access; page dated 2021.
- d:N24. DAC: the resource owner decides, using access control lists. Everyday example: the sharing settings on a file you own. Downsides: nested groups create over or under privilege, and to see one user's rights you must search every ACL.
  - Source: https://www.twingate.com/blog/other/access-control-models  Quote: "The sharing option in most operating systems is a form of DAC."
  - Kind: definition
  - Level: core
- d:N25. RBAC: rights follow the job. Example that also teaches separation of duties: accounts payable clerks can create payments but not approve them; their supervisor can approve but not create.
  - Source: https://www.twingate.com/blog/other/access-control-models  Quote: "Supervisors, on the other hand, can approve payments but may not create them."
  - Kind: story
  - Level: core
- d:N26. RBAC's trap: too many overlapping roles per user brings back over-privilege; defining roles in a big firm is hard enough to have its own name, "role engineering".
  - Source: https://www.twingate.com/blog/other/access-control-models  Quote: "Assigning too many roles to users also increases the risk of over-privileging users."
  - Kind: mistake
  - Level: core
- d:N27. Privileged access management gives admins limited, temporary rights when needed, removes shared passwords, and stops "permission creep" (collecting rights over time). Cited figure: 53% of organizations had privileged credentials stolen, 85% of those led to breaches of critical systems (their claim, citing a ThycoticCentrify study).
  - Source: https://www.twingate.com/blog/other/access-control-models  Quote: "preventing users from collecting access privileges"
  - Kind: number
  - Level: core
  - Bias: vendor. FLAG: PAM is a practice for privileged accounts, not one of the standard access control models, and the page omits ABAC.
- d:N28. Context-aware access (the road to ABAC and zero trust): decide each connection on device posture, location (office LAN vs café hotspot) and behavior, a per-user, per-device, per-context risk check.
  - Source: https://www.twingate.com/blog/other/access-control-models  Quote: "a per-device, per-user, per-context risk assessment with every connection attempt"
  - Kind: definition
  - Level: core
  - Also: T12
  - Bias: vendor pitch for its zero trust product.

## T11 Threats and attacks
- d:N29. Virus vs worm: a virus attaches to legitimate files and needs a person to spread it; a worm self-replicates across a network through vulnerabilities with no human action.
  - Source: https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/what-is/endpoint-security/types-of-malware.html  Quote: "Unlike viruses, worms do not need human interaction to spread."
  - Kind: definition
  - Level: foundation
  - Bias: Trend Micro sells endpoint security.
- d:N30. Melissa (1999) spread through Word and Outlook email attachments, crashed servers, about $1.1 billion in damages (their claim). Viruses dominated until about 2000, which is why the industry is still called "antivirus".
  - Source: https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/what-is/endpoint-security/types-of-malware.html  Quote: "leading to the industry combating malicious code to be called Antivirus"
  - Kind: story
  - Level: foundation
- d:N31. WannaCry (2017) was a worm that also encrypted data for ransom: one example sits in two categories. Worms are rarely used in targeted attacks because they are noisy and hard for attackers to control.
  - Source: https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/what-is/endpoint-security/types-of-malware.html  Quote: "very hard to control for threat actors leading to unintended side effects"
  - Kind: story
  - Level: foundation
- d:N32. Trojan disguises itself as legitimate software (Zeus, since 2007, stole banking credentials); RATs give remote control. Rootkit hides privileged long-term access; the Sony BMG CD copy protection rootkit (2005) is the famous case of a company shipping one.
  - Source: https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/what-is/endpoint-security/types-of-malware.html  Quote: "rootkit software was secretly installed on CDs to prevent copying"
  - Kind: story
  - Level: foundation
- d:N33. Botnet: a remotely controlled army of "zombie" devices. Mirai (2016) used one for a DDoS that took major websites down. Ransomware as a Service groups behind nearly 1,500 successful attacks on EU businesses, July 2023 to June 2024 (their claim, citing ENISA).
  - Source: https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/what-is/endpoint-security/types-of-malware.html  Quote: "a network of compromised devices, also known as zombies"
  - Kind: story
  - Level: foundation
- d:N34. Cryptojacking: mining on someone else's machine without consent; it steals energy and is often ignored as minor. Spyware (Pegasus, 2016), adware (Fireball, 2017) and keyloggers round out the list.
  - Source: https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/what-is/endpoint-security/types-of-malware.html  Quote: "Stealing Energie is often regarded as a minor issue and therefore ignored."
  - Kind: definition
  - Level: foundation
- d:N35. Four baseline defenses against malware: patch everything, firewalls, train staff to spot phishing, endpoint detection and response (EDR).
  - Source: https://www.trendmicro.com/en_us/what-is/endpoint-security/types-of-malware.html  Quote: "Software updates will often contain patches"
  - Kind: rule of thumb
  - Level: foundation
  - Also: T17

## T13 Cloud: service models, shared responsibility
- d:N36. Shared responsibility: the provider secures physical infrastructure and the virtualization layer; the customer secures configuration, identity and access policies, monitoring and its own apps and data.
  - Source: https://searchcloudcomputing.techtarget.com/feature/The-cloud-shared-responsibility-model-for-IaaS-PaaS-and-SaaS  Quote: "Cloud customers are responsible for managing the resources they control, and the CSP does the rest."
  - Kind: definition
  - Level: foundation
- d:N37. The split moves with the model: IaaS, provider does hardware and virtualization, you do everything else; PaaS, provider also runs OS and dev tooling, you own your apps; SaaS, provider runs almost everything, you still own settings and data.
  - Source: https://searchcloudcomputing.techtarget.com/feature/The-cloud-shared-responsibility-model-for-IaaS-PaaS-and-SaaS  Quote: "PaaS is the middle ground in the cloud shared responsibility model"
  - Kind: template
  - Level: foundation
- d:N38. PaaS scenario: a customer picks a weak password, an attacker logs in, injects code and deploys the bad app. The breach is the customer's fault even though the provider runs the platform.
  - Source: https://searchcloudcomputing.techtarget.com/feature/The-cloud-shared-responsibility-model-for-IaaS-PaaS-and-SaaS  Quote: "the customer would be responsible for allowing the breach"
  - Kind: story
  - Level: foundation
  - Also: T08
- d:N39. SaaS mistakes stay yours: making a document public is your config error, and SaaS vendors usually will not restore data you deleted yourself, so back it up.
  - Source: https://searchcloudcomputing.techtarget.com/feature/The-cloud-shared-responsibility-model-for-IaaS-PaaS-and-SaaS  Quote: "they usually won't recover data that customers accidentally delete"
  - Kind: mistake
  - Level: foundation
  - Also: T07
- d:N40. SLA questions to ask a provider: what is guaranteed, how downtime is measured, what you get if it is missed (maybe nothing), track record, and whether the provider can change terms on its own.
  - Source: https://searchcloudcomputing.techtarget.com/feature/The-cloud-shared-responsibility-model-for-IaaS-PaaS-and-SaaS  Quote: "the SLA agreement includes no compensation"
  - Kind: template
  - Level: core

## T14 Cryptography basics
- d:N41. Hashing is one-way and fixed length, a fingerprint; encryption is reversible with a key. Hashing serves integrity, encryption serves confidentiality. SHA-256 is a hash, not encryption.
  - Source: https://rippling.com/blog/hashing-vs-encryption  Quote: "SHA256 is a hashing algorithm, not encryption."
  - Kind: definition
  - Level: foundation
  - Also: T02
  - Bias: Rippling sells IT and identity software.
- d:N42. Avalanche effect: change one letter of input and the whole hash changes. Same input always gives the same hash, which is how a download checksum proves a file is unchanged.
  - Source: https://rippling.com/blog/hashing-vs-encryption  Quote: "Change just one letter in a document, and the entire hash changes dramatically."
  - Kind: test
  - Level: foundation
- d:N43. Mistake both ways: passwords stored with reversible encryption fall if the key leaks; data you need back that was "protected" by hashing is gone. Store passwords as salted, slow hashes (bcrypt, Argon2); MD5 and SHA-1 are broken for security use.
  - Source: https://rippling.com/blog/hashing-vs-encryption  Quote: "Passwords stored with reversible encryption can be cracked if keys are compromised."
  - Kind: mistake
  - Level: core
  - Also: T08
- d:N44. Symmetric uses one shared key, fast, but the key must be shared safely (AES); asymmetric uses a public and private pair, solves key sharing but is slower (RSA, ECC). Digital signature: hash the document, sign the hash with the private key, anyone verifies with the public key, giving integrity and origin.
  - Source: https://rippling.com/blog/hashing-vs-encryption  Quote: "the hash of a document is encrypted with a private key"
  - Kind: definition
  - Level: foundation
  - FLAG: the page lists DES beside AES as "common" symmetric algorithms without noting DES is obsolete and insecure.
